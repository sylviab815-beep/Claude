# Cozy Mystery Prose Craft — 10 Rules

Apply these while drafting and re-check them in editorial Stage 1. They are checkpoints, not suggestions. The biggest quality gap in AI cozy mystery is prose that *tells* instead of *shows* — naming emotions, explaining the world, and stating themes in dialogue instead of letting them live on the page.

## Rule 1: Emotions Are Physical, Not Named
Never write "I felt anxious" or "fear coursed through her." Show what the feeling does to the body — in a cozy this is gentler but still specific: damp palms around a warm mug, a scone that won't go down, the cat pressing closer because it senses something's off.
**Check:** If you can delete a sentence and the reader still knows the emotion from the surrounding action, it was telling. Delete it.

## Rule 2: World-Building Through Daily Life, Not Explanation
Readers learn the town (and, in paranormal mode, the magic) by living in it. Don't explain that the bakery's pastries are enchanted — show a bite of muffin pull up a grandmother's kitchen in perfect detail. Don't lecture the rules of psychometry — show the sleuth flinch when she touches the wrong doorknob.
**Check:** If a character explains something both characters already know, it's info-dumping. Cut or convert to demonstration.

## Rule 3: Theme Lives in Action, Not Dialogue
Characters never say the theme out loud. No "maybe starting over isn't about forgetting where you've been." Theme emerges from choices, costs, and small acts of courage.
**Check:** Search dialogue for abstract nouns (belonging, trust, second chances, home). If characters discuss the concept, rewrite so they live it.

## Rule 4: Clues Through Observation, Not Announcement
The sleuth notices — a scent that's wrong, a reaction a half-second too fast, a detail that doesn't match. She files it; the reader files it. She does not say "that's suspicious!" In paranormal mode, the familiar may notice what she misses and report it through an animal lens she then has to decode.
**Check:** Every planted clue should read as natural observation, never as a flagged "clue moment."

## Rule 5: The Sleuth's Voice Is Specific and Lived-In
Most cozy sleuths are women with real history (often midlife). Her internal voice has the texture of someone who's survived things — references from her era, dry self-awareness, quiet competence. If her narration could belong to anyone of any age, it isn't specific enough.
**Check:** Could you identify the narrator from a single paragraph with no name attached? You should be able to.

## Rule 6: Dialogue Is Behavior, Not Information
Characters talk to get something — answers, comfort, a laugh, a reaction. They interrupt, deflect, change the subject. They never patiently recite town history or articulate their emotional state. Each recurring character is identifiable by speech alone.
**Check:** Read any exchange. Is anyone delivering exposition they wouldn't deliver in real life? Rewrite it as behavior.

## Rule 7: Comfort Is Texture, Not Filler
Food, drink, weather, animals, seasonal detail, the rhythm of opening the shop — these are the product, not padding. Render them with specific sensory detail (the particular crackle of a sugar crust, not "delicious pastries").
**Check:** Does every 3-chapter stretch contain at least one genuine comfort beat that's specific, not generic?

## Rule 8: Restraint at the Body
The discovery scene carries weight through restraint, not gore. Focus on the sleuth's human reaction and the community's shock — a still hand, an overturned chair, the wrongness of silence — not wounds.
**Check:** Is there any lingering description of injury or violence? Pull it back.

## Rule 9: Humor From Character and Situation
The comedy is a warm, wry worldview, not a joke-per-line — the absurdity of solving a murder while the shop's espresso machine dies, the familiar judging everyone, the townsfolk's small vanities. It should make the reader smile, and earn at least one real laugh per few chapters, without ever turning farcical.
**Check:** Does the humor come from who these people are, or is it generic "witty narration" anyone could deliver?

## Rule 10: Scenes Open in Motion, End on Pull
Open in the middle of something happening — not waking up, not a recap paragraph (unless the familiar is doing something ridiculous that drags the sleuth out of bed). End chapters on forward momentum — a question, a decision, a new wrinkle — never on a one-line dramatic mic-drop or a philosophical wrap-up.
**Check:** Does the chapter start with action and end pulling the reader into the next, without manufactured cliffhangers?

## Cozy AI Clichés to Avoid (and replace)

- "A chill ran down her spine." / "Her breath hitched." / "Her blood ran cold."
- "Little did she know…" / "If only she'd known…" (POV-breaking foreshadow)
- "Something about him seemed off." (vague intuition — name the specific tell)
- "The air of someone who…" / "the kind of [noun] that…" (distancing constructions)
- "She couldn't help but…" / "She found herself [verb]-ing." (removes agency)
- Three-beat description lists in every paragraph (AI defaults to exactly three).
- The cozy town being "charming" and "quaint" *told* rather than rendered.
- Every scene ending on a dramatic one-liner.
- The familiar narrating the solution; the cat being named Salem/Binx; the town named Willowbrook.
